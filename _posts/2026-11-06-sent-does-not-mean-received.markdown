There is a particular kind of frustration that comes from discovering that something important never landed. You sent the message. You assigned the task. You left the comment. You did your part. And then, weeks later, you find out the other person had no idea what you needed from them.

The instinct is to blame the receiver. They weren't paying attention. They should have followed up. This is the wrong diagnosis.

Sent does not mean received. Received does not mean understood. Understood does not mean acted on. These are four separate things, and most teams conflate all of them.

---

The gap between sent and received is where most team dysfunction lives, and it's almost invisible until something goes wrong.

A message in Slack disappears into a busy channel. A task is assigned but the assignee has twelve other things open and doesn't register the priority. An email arrives in a full inbox and gets mentally filed under "I'll deal with this later" and then forgotten. A PR review request goes out and the reviewer sees the notification but doesn't act on it, because they're mid-context on something else.

In each of these cases, the sender believes the transfer has happened. The receiver either didn't see it, saw it but didn't process it, or processed it but didn't understand what action was expected. The sender has moved on. The receiver is still holding nothing.

The result is invisible to both parties until it surfaces as a missed deadline, a wrong assumption, or a conversation that begins with "I thought you were handling that."

---

The fix is not to send more messages. It is to design for confirmation.

Confirmation is not a bureaucratic nicety. It is the mechanism that closes the loop. When someone confirms they've received and understood a message, you know the transfer happened. When they don't, you know to follow up. The absence of confirmation is information.

This is what "sent does not mean received" means in practice: the responsibility for a task or decision does not leave your hands until you have a receipt. A read receipt is not enough. An acknowledgment is not enough. You need confirmation that the other person understands what is expected of them and accepts the responsibility.

This sounds like overhead. In teams that already communicate well, it barely registers — the confirmation happens naturally as part of the reply. In teams that don't, it's the difference between things getting done and things falling silently through the gaps.

---

Applied to software, the principle runs through everything.

A PR description that doesn't get a response is not a reviewed PR. A spec that nobody pushed back on is not a confirmed spec. A deploy that happened without a status update in the team channel is a deploy that nobody knows happened. A handoff without a "got it" is not a handoff — it's a transfer of hope.

The paper trail matters too. If the confirmation happened in a meeting or a hallway conversation, it didn't happen in a way that anyone can check later. Write it down. The conversation is not the record. The written summary of the conversation is the record.

Spoken words are gone the moment they're said. Written words survive. When there's ambiguity about what was agreed — and there will be ambiguity — the written record is what you go back to. If you didn't write it down, you're asking people to reconstruct a conversation from memory, which is to say you're asking them to guess.

---

The cultural version of this is that getting confirmation requires making it safe to not confirm.

If your team has any social cost attached to saying "I don't understand" or "I haven't looked at that yet," people will fake confirmation to avoid that cost. They will say they got the message when they didn't fully process it. They will nod in the meeting rather than admit confusion. They will mark a task as acknowledged and then quietly let it sit.

A team that punishes honest uncertainty will manufacture false certainty. And false certainty is worse than no certainty, because it closes the loop that was never actually closed. You think you have a receipt. You don't.

Build the environment where "I haven't read it yet" and "I don't understand what you need" are normal, low-friction responses. The alternative is a team that tells you what you want to hear and then doesn't deliver.

---

The rule is simple. Before you move on, make sure the other side has confirmed. Not just that they saw it — that they understood it and know what's expected.

If you're waiting on that confirmation and it hasn't come, follow up. Not aggressively. Just: "Did this land?" That single question closes more open loops than any amount of process documentation.

Sent does not mean received. Received does not mean understood. Understood does not mean done. Know which stage you're at before you assume the work is in motion.
